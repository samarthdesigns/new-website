TLDR

  • What problem did I solve?

    On a Google Meet call, the call lives in one tab and I live in all the others. Meet's pop-out window fixes that by covering the thing I'm actually working on.

  • What was my role?

    Everything. I did the concept, interaction design, motion and brand. I also built it with Claude as my engineer and directed and tested every build.

  • What decisions did I make?

    I split the call in two. A small island at the top of the screen holds the controls and only opens on hover. Speaker bubbles in the corner show who's talking: the speaker as video, everyone else as small faces. Move your cursor near them and they slide away.

  • What was the outcome?

    A Chrome extension, now in Chrome Web Store review, and a free desktop app for Windows, macOS and Linux. It runs entirely on your computer: no accounts, no servers.

01 / 04

Where it came from

I spend half my day on calls and the other half pretending I'm not on one. The call sits in one tab; my doc, my Figma file and my inbox sit in all the others.

Meet, Teams and Zoom all have an answer for this: the pop-out. It's a full window with a grid of faces, parked on top of whatever I'm doing. It's always in the wrong spot, and it's always the wrong size.

So I kept tabbing back to the call just to see who was talking, and missed what they said on the way there.

The insight

I don't need to see everyone. I need to know who's talking.

A call grid gives everyone the same space. In practice, one person talks and the rest listen. The pop-out spends most of its pixels on people who aren't saying anything.

Controls are for moments, not for always.

Mute, camera, raise hand, react, leave: I use each of them a few times a call. They don't need to be on screen the other 59 minutes.

The decision

The design question was how small a call can get before it stops being useful.

The answer was to stop treating it as a window. A window has edges, a title bar and a size you manage. PopTalk has two pieces that float over everything and ask for nothing until you need them.

The island

It sits at the top centre, the one spot no app uses. At rest it shows the speaker's name and a voice meter. Hover and it opens into the full set of call controls.

The bubbles

They sit in the corner. The speaker gets a video tile; recent speakers stay as small bubbles, five at most; everyone else folds into "+3". The idea came from Discord's voice bubbles.

What was shipped

The island and the bubbles, on top of every app, driven by the real Meet call in your browser.

Who's talking, at a glance.

The speaker's bubble grows into live video and the others shrink back. When someone new starts talking, the bubbles trade places on a spring, so the change catches your eye without pulling you out of what you're doing.

One island for every control.

Mic, camera, screen share, reactions, raise hand, captions and leave. It's black and small, and it only opens on hover.

Chat pops in where you work.

New messages show up under the bubbles with the sender's name, then fade. There's no panel to open and no tab to find.

Screen shares land in the big bubble.

When someone shares, their screen takes the video tile so you can follow along without switching.

Out of your way, literally.

Move your cursor near the bubbles and they slide clear and fade to a ghost, so you can click whatever was underneath. Move away and they come back. This was the detail that made it feel like a layer rather than a window.

Yours to place.

Hold Alt (Option on a Mac) to drag the bubbles anywhere or resize them.

01 / 11

Private by design.

PopTalk reads the call on your computer and draws it on your computer. Nothing gets sent anywhere: no accounts, no servers, no analytics.

The hard part, in one line

A browser allows only one floating window at a time, and I wanted two pieces on screen. So PopTalk is two parts: a Chrome extension that reads the Meet call, and a small desktop app that draws the island and the bubbles over everything. Without the app, the extension still works in a single floating window.
